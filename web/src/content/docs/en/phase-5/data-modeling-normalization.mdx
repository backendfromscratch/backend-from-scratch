## The problem

Imagine that, before having a database, Ana ran the shop in a spreadsheet, with one row per order:

| order_no | product | price_cents | categories | quantity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mug | 850 | Home, Gifts | 2 |
| 2 | Hoodie | 3500 | Clothing | 3 |
| 4 | Mug | 850 | Home, Gifts | 1 |

One day she raises the mug to €9. She changes the price in order 4, the one in front of her, and forgets about order 1. Now the sheet says the mug costs €8.50 and €9 at the same time, and she doesn't know which one is right.

In lessons 1 to 3, the tables were already designed. Now you design them: which tables, which columns and which rules. That's the **data model**, and everything else is built on top of it. Changing it once the shop is full of data costs much more than thinking it through now.

## The analogy

<Analogy>

Think of a wedding. The address book has each guest's address, just once. The seating plan (“Table 3: Marta and Pablo”) and the gift list (“Marta: the coffee maker”) only have names.

If Marta moves, you change one line in the address book and everything else is still right. If you had copied her address onto every piece of paper, you'd have to find all the copies, and the one you miss will send something to her old house.

<div slot="limits">

- The address book doesn't stop you from seating someone who isn't in it. A database does, with the rules in this lesson.
- Sometimes copying is the right thing: the invitation you already sent has the address from back then, and that's how it should stay. The same goes for the price of an order, as you'll see at the end.

## How it really works

The outputs are from PostgreSQL 18.6, with the shop's data. You can try the queries in `psql -d shop` or in the playground in [Try it](#try-it).

### First, the things and how they relate

Modeling starts on paper. Write down the things the shop keeps data about, the **entities**: products, categories and orders. Each entity will be a table, and each piece of its data, a column.

Next, how they relate. For each pair, ask yourself how many of one the other can have, both ways:

| Pair | One way | The other way | Relationship | Where it's stored |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product and order | An order is for one product | A product has many orders | One to many (1:N) | `product_id`, in `orders` |
| Product and category | A product is in several categories | A category has several products | Many to many (N:M) | An intermediate table, `product_categories` |

### One to many: the key goes on the many side

In a 1:N relationship, the <Term id="foreign-key">foreign key</Term> from [lesson 2](/en/phase-5/sql-joins/) goes in the table on the many side: each order stores its `product_id`. The other way around doesn't fit, because a product with 9 orders would need 9 values in one cell.

### Many to many: an intermediate table

With products and categories, neither side has a single value: the Mug is in Home and in Gifts, and Clothing has three products. The first two ideas fail:

- **A `categories` column with `'Home, Gifts'`:** it's text, and PostgreSQL neither checks that those categories exist nor can join it with a JOIN.
- **Columns `category1` and `category2`:** a third category forces you to change the table, and to find the products in Gifts you have to look in both.

The solution is another table, with **one row per pair**: `product_categories`, with `(2, 2)` and `(2, 3)` for the Mug. It's the one you joined with two JOINs in [lesson 2](/en/phase-5/sql-joins/).

### The schema: the tables, written in SQL

A database's <Term id="database-schema">schema</Term> is the list of its tables, with their columns, their types and their rules. It's written with `CREATE TABLE`. This is the one for products, in your project:

Word by word:

- `CREATE TABLE products (…)` creates the table, with one column per line, separated by commas: its name and its type.
- `id integer GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY` is the primary key from [lesson 2](/en/phase-5/sql-joins/).
- `NOT NULL` makes the column required: the `NULL` from [lesson 1](/en/phase-5/what-is-sql/) doesn't get in.
- `CHECK (price_cents > 0)` is a condition every row has to meet.
- `--` starts a comment, which PostgreSQL ignores.

Don't confuse it with the Zod schema from [Phase 3](/en/phase-3/zod-validation/), which describes a JSON.

### Constraints: rules that PostgreSQL always checks

`NOT NULL` and `CHECK` are <Term id="constraint">constraints</Term>: rules the database checks on every `INSERT` and every `UPDATE`. Categories have another one:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/18-modeling/db/schema.sql" region="categories" />

`UNIQUE` forbids repeating a value in the column: there can't be two “Clothing” categories. Orders bring almost all of them together:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/18-modeling/db/schema.sql" region="orders" />

- `REFERENCES products (id)` is the foreign key: only the id of a product that exists is valid.
- `CHECK (quantity > 0)`: no order of zero units.
- `CHECK (status IN (…))`: `IN` means “one of these.”

An order of zero units doesn't get in:

```sql
INSERT INTO orders (product_id, quantity, status, created_at)
VALUES (2, 0, 'pending', now());
```

```
ERROR:  new row for relation "orders" violates check constraint "orders_quantity_check"
DETAIL:  Failing row contains (26, 2, 0, pending, 2026-10-13 23:46:07.403364+00).
```

“The new row in `orders` breaks the constraint `orders_quantity_check`,” a name PostgreSQL builds from the table and the column.

**Why in the database, if the API already validates with Zod?** Because the API isn't the only one that writes. A script that imports orders, someone in `psql` or a bug in the code all skip Zod, but not PostgreSQL. Zod gives the client a good message; the constraint is the last door.

### ON DELETE: what happens when you delete something others point to

In [lesson 2](/en/phase-5/sql-joins/), deleting the Mug failed because its orders point to it. When you declare a foreign key, you choose what happens in that case:

| You write | When the product is deleted, the rows that point to it… |
|---|---|
| Nothing (or `ON DELETE RESTRICT`) | …prevent it: the delete fails with an error |
| `ON DELETE CASCADE` | …are deleted with it |
| `ON DELETE SET NULL` | …are left with `NULL` in that column, if it allows it |

The project chooses differently in each table:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/18-modeling/db/schema.sql" region="product-categories" />

- **`product_categories`, with `CASCADE`.** “The Cap is in Gifts” means nothing without the Cap: if the product is deleted, its pairs go with it. The category stays.
- **`orders`, without `ON DELETE`.** The orders are the shop's history. Deleting a product can't delete its sales.
- `PRIMARY KEY (product_id, category_id)` is the two-column primary key from lesson 2: the same pair can't get in twice.

With these rules, the Cap, which has no orders, is deleted along with its two categories; the Mug isn't:

```sql
DELETE FROM products WHERE id = 4;
DELETE FROM products WHERE id = 2;
```

```
DELETE 1
ERROR:  update or delete on table "products" violates foreign key constraint "orders_product_id_fkey" on table "orders"
DETAIL:  Key (id)=(2) is still referenced from table "orders".
```

In [lesson 5](/en/phase-5/node-postgres/), the API will turn that error into a `409`.

### Normalizing: each piece of data in one place

Go back to Ana's spreadsheet, now as a PostgreSQL table, `sheet`, with the three rows from “The problem.” The half-done price change:

```sql
UPDATE sheet SET price_cents = 900 WHERE order_no = 4;
SELECT order_no, product, price_cents FROM sheet WHERE product = 'Mug';
```

```
UPDATE 1
 order_no | product | price_cents
----------+---------+-------------
        1 | Mug     |         850
        4 | Mug     |         900
(2 rows)
```

It's the first of three **anomalies**, errors caused by the design itself:

- **On update:** a piece of data copied into several rows ends up with two values.
- **On insert:** the Cap, which nobody has ordered, doesn't fit. Each row is an order, and without an order the key is missing: `INSERT INTO sheet (product, price_cents) VALUES ('Cap', 1500);` gives `ERROR:  null value in column "order_no"…`.
- **On delete:** if order 2 is deleted, the Hoodie's only one, the shop forgets that it exists and what it costs.

All three have the same cause: the sheet mixes orders, products and categories in one table. <Term id="normalization">Normalization</Term> separates them so that **each piece of data is stored in a single place**. Its rules are called normal forms.

### The three normal forms, in words

| Normal form | The rule | Broken by | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| First (1NF) | Each cell holds a single value | `categories` with `'Home, Gifts'` | One row per pair, in `product_categories` |
| Second (2NF) | If the key has several columns, each piece of data depends on all of it | A `product_name` in `product_categories`: it depends only on `product_id` | The name, in `products` |
| Third (3NF) | Each piece of data depends on the key, not on another column | The price in the sheet: it depends on the product, not on the order | The price, in `products` |

William Kent summed it up in 1983: each piece of data depends **on the key, the whole key and nothing but the key**. The result is the project's four tables. The price lives in `products` and each category in `categories`. The order stores only what's its own: which product, how many, in what status and when.

There are more normal forms, stricter ones. With the third, almost every application does fine.

### Denormalizing: copying on purpose

Sometimes it pays to copy a piece of data on purpose: that's **denormalizing**. There are two typical cases.

**The price at the time.** The shop's orders don't store the price. The revenue report from [lesson 3](/en/phase-5/sql-group-by/) multiplies each quantity by today's price:

```sql
SELECT p.name, SUM(o.quantity * p.price_cents) AS revenue_cents
FROM orders AS o
JOIN products AS p ON p.id = o.product_id
WHERE p.name = 'Mug'
GROUP BY p.id, p.name;
```

```
 name | revenue_cents
------+---------------
 Mug  |         18700
(1 row)
```

Ana raises the mug to €9 with `UPDATE products SET price_cents = 900 WHERE name = 'Mug';`, and the same query gives `19800`. The 22 mugs were charged at €8.50, but the report says they brought in €198: **it has changed the past**. That's why real shops store in each order the price it was sold at. It looks like a copy, but it's a different piece of data: what was charged, which doesn't change with the product.

The course's shop doesn't store it, so the orders stay as they were in the Phase 4 API, and that's why its reports use today's price. Adding it would be a new field in the order's JSON, a change that doesn't break anything.

**A stored total.** A `total_cents` column in each order saves the multiplication when reading. But if someone changes the quantity and forgets the total, the database contradicts itself. Only copy when you've measured that reading is slow ([lesson 7](/en/phase-5/database-indexes-explain/) teaches you how to measure it).

The rule: **normalize by default, and denormalize with a reason**, knowing which copy can go stale.

### An order with several products would break the API

In the course's shop, an order is for a single product. A real shop lets you buy a mug and a cap together. Then `orders` would be left with `status` and `created_at`, and a new table, `order_lines`, would have one row per product in each order: another 1:N relationship. And along with the tables, the JSON would change:

```text
Before: {"id":26,"productId":2,"quantity":1,"status":"pending",…}
After:  {"id":26,"lines":[{"productId":2,"quantity":1},{"productId":4,"quantity":1}],"status":"pending",…}
```

The delivery company reads `productId` and `quantity`, and they'd be gone. It's a <Term id="breaking-change">breaking change</Term>: it would need a `/v3` ([lesson 4 of Phase 4](/en/phase-4/api-versioning/)). That's why, in this phase, the shop keeps one product per order and its API only grows by adding. **The data model ends up being the shape of your API**: think it through before others depend on it.

<SelfCheck question="Challenge: design the reviews table. Each review is of one product, with a required rating from 1 to 5 and a required text. What happens to the reviews if their product is deleted?">

It's a 1:N relationship, so the foreign key goes in `reviews`:

```sql
CREATE TABLE reviews (
  id integer GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
  product_id integer NOT NULL REFERENCES products (id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
  rating integer NOT NULL CHECK (rating >= 1 AND rating <= 5),
  body text NOT NULL
);
INSERT INTO reviews (product_id, rating, body) VALUES (2, 6, 'The best mug');
```

```
CREATE TABLE
ERROR:  new row for relation "reviews" violates check constraint "reviews_rating_check"
DETAIL:  Failing row contains (1, 2, 6, The best mug).
```

A review makes no sense without its product, so `CASCADE` is reasonable; leaving out `ON DELETE` wouldn't be wrong either. Delete it afterward with `DROP TABLE reviews;`.

</SelfCheck>

## You have already seen it

- **A Google Form with checkboxes** stores “Home, Gifts” in a single cell of its responses sheet: the first normal form, broken.
- **A store receipt** has the price you paid, even if it goes up tomorrow: the price at the time, copied on purpose.
- **“This email is already registered”** when you create an account: behind it there's usually a `UNIQUE` column.

## Common mistakes

- **Storing a list in a cell.** Use an intermediate table.
- **Validating only in the API.** Any other program, or you in `psql`, writes without going through it.
- **`ON DELETE CASCADE` on every foreign key.** Deleting a product would delete its sales.
- **Copying the product's name into each order “to save the JOIN.”** When it's renamed, the orders contradict each other.
- **Not storing the price it was sold at**, if your reports need it: every price rise changes the past.

## Summary

- Modeling starts with the entities and their relationships: in a 1:N, the foreign key goes on the many side; an N:M needs an intermediate table.
- The schema is written with `CREATE TABLE`, and its constraints stop bad data, wherever it comes from.
- `ON DELETE` decides what happens when you delete something others point to.
- Normalizing is storing each piece of data in a single place, to avoid the anomalies on update, insert and delete.
- Denormalizing is copying on purpose, with a reason and knowing which copy can go stale.

The first normal form asks for a single value per cell, and here each cell has one.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>

  question="Ana raises the Hoodie from €35 to €40. What happens to the revenue per product report from lesson 3?"
  review="Denormalizing: copying on purpose"
>

<QuizOption text="It goes up: the report multiplies by today’s price." correct>

The orders don't store the price, so the 20 hoodies sold now count at €40.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Nothing: each of the shop’s orders stores the price that was charged.">

The course's orders store `product_id`, `quantity`, `status` and `created_at`. The price is in `products`.

</QuizOption>

The foreign key protects the product's `id`, not its price.

</QuizOption>
