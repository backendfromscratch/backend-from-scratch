## The problem

Imagine a Tuesday at eleven at night. PostgreSQL stops answering, and the shop answers `500` to every sign-in. You’re having dinner. You find out the next morning, from a customer’s email: “I couldn’t sign in last night.”

The dashboard from [lesson 5](/en/phase-10/prometheus-grafana-metrics/) showed it, but nobody looks at a dashboard at eleven at night. **The shop needs to come looking for you**: an alert that writes to you when errors go past a limit. [Phase 6](/en/phase-6/owasp-top-10/#logs-the-event-yes-the-secret-never) left it pending: a log nobody reads warns of nothing.

And before that, something more basic: “should I restart this copy?” and “should I send it customers?” are two different questions, and answering both with `/health` is expensive.

## The analogy

<Analogy>

A restaurant has two questions that aren’t the same: is the cook OK? And can the kitchen serve dishes?

If the gas is cut off, the cook is perfectly fine, but can’t cook. The sensible thing is to hang “closed for a while” on the door, so nobody comes in to wait, and call the gas technician. Calling an ambulance for the cook doesn’t fix the gas. If the dishwasher breaks, on the other hand, they keep serving: more slowly, washing by hand.

And nobody waits for a customer to complain about the smoke: the detector beeps at the smoke, whether it comes from a frying pan or a cable.

<div slot="limits">

- In the restaurant, someone sees there’s no gas. In the shop, whoever hands out the requests only knows what the check answers.
- The shop can have many copies, and a PostgreSQL outage reaches all of them at once: restarting them all is much worse than one ambulance too many.
- A smoke detector comes with its limit from the factory. You choose your alerts’ limits, and they get measured: a copied limit alerts too much or too little.

</div>

</Analogy>

The cook is `/health/live`; the kitchen that can serve, `/health/ready`; the dishwasher, the cache, and the smoke detector, an alert on what customers notice.

## How it really works

### Two questions, two routes

In [Phase 9](/en/phase-9/zero-downtime-deployment/#alive-and-ready-liveness-and-readiness) you saw the two questions: is the copy alive (*liveness*)? and is it ready to serve (*readiness*)? Step 46 gives each one a route:

- **`/health/live`**, the <Term id="liveness-probe">liveness probe</Term>: if it doesn’t answer, the copy is restarted.
- **`/health/ready`**, the <Term id="readiness-probe">readiness probe</Term>: if it says `503`, requests stop being sent to it, without restarting it.

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/src/app.ts" region="live" />

**It asks nobody, on purpose.** If it looked at PostgreSQL, a database outage would make every copy’s probe fail at once, and they’d all be restarted. Restarting doesn’t fix PostgreSQL: it only adds copies starting up to an outage that was already there.

### /health/ready asks three, half a second each

It asks each dependency and says what it saw:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/src/health.ts" region="ready" />

- **All three at once** (`Promise.all`), and **each one with 500 ms at most** (`check`, in the same file). A frozen dependency never answers, and whoever asks needs an answer.
- **It fails if PostgreSQL or the queue’s Redis fails**: without them there’s no shop, no sign-ins and no jobs.
- **The cache is reported, but doesn’t count**: without it, the shop reads from PostgreSQL, [more slowly](/en/phase-9/redis-cache/#if-the-cache-fails-the-shop-keeps-going). It’s the dishwasher.

### /health stays as it was

It’s read by the Dockerfile’s <Term id="healthcheck">healthcheck</Term>, since [Phase 7](/en/phase-7/docker-multi-stage-build/#healthcheck-is-the-shop-fine), and by `deploy.sh`. For them, “healthy” is: it reaches PostgreSQL and it isn’t shutting down. If it were `/health/ready`, a Redis outage would roll back a deployment that’s not to blame; if it were `/health/live`, a copy with no database would get through.

And who reads `/health/ready`? In your Compose, nobody: Docker has a single healthcheck, and nginx [only looks at the real requests](/en/phase-8/nginx-reverse-proxy/#two-copies-and-nginx-finds-out-on-its-own). The new routes are for an orchestrator like Kubernetes, which asks each question separately. **A probe is only useful if someone does the right thing with its answer.**

### SLI, SLO and error budget

Before alerting, you have to decide what “going well” is. In the shop:

- **The <Term id="sli">SLI</Term>**, what’s measured: the share of `/v2`’s responses that aren’t a 5xx.
- **The <Term id="slo">SLO</Term>**, the target: 99%, counted over 30 days.
- **The <Term id="error-budget">error budget</Term>**, the 1% left over: with a million requests a month, 10,000 errors.

**While there’s budget left, new features get deployed; if it runs out, it’s time to fix reliability.** And it gives an alert its measure: with a 5% error rate that lasted, the whole month’s budget would run out in six days (30 divided by 5). That’s no longer bad luck.

### Alerts on symptoms, not on causes

A **cause** is what can break something: the CPU at 90%, a Redis that’s down. A **symptom** is what whoever uses the shop notices: errors, slowness, an email that doesn’t arrive.

**The shop alerts on symptoms.** A symptom alerts you of any cause, including the ones nobody had thought of, and a cause nobody notices isn’t worth waking anyone up for. Grafana loads four rules from files in the step; asked through its API:

```text
{"title":"5xx errors on /v2","for":"2m","noDataState":"OK","severity":"urgent"}
{"title":"High p99 on /v2","for":"2m","noDataState":"OK","severity":"warning"}
{"title":"Outgoing events stuck","for":"0s","noDataState":"OK","severity":"urgent"}
{"title":"The API is not sending metrics","for":"1m","noDataState":"Alerting","severity":"urgent"}
```

The first three are symptoms: errors, slowness and emails that don’t go out. The fourth watches the others.

### A rule from the inside: query, last value and threshold

The errors one, in `rules.yaml`, has three steps. The first, **A**, the query to Prometheus:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/observability/grafana/provisioning/alerting/rules.yaml" region="errors-query" />

What share of `/v2`’s responses were 5xx in the last minute, with the `rate` from [lesson 5](/en/phase-10/prometheus-grafana-metrics/#promql-in-four-queries): the SLI seen the other way around, the share that fails. Then, the threshold:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/observability/grafana/provisioning/alerting/rules.yaml" region="errors-threshold" />

- **B** keeps A’s last value, and **C** checks whether it goes over `0.05`, 5%.
- **`for: 2m`**, in the list above: Grafana evaluates it every 10 seconds. When it’s met, it goes to **pending**; if it keeps being met for 2 minutes, to **firing**, and it alerts. A lone error wakes nobody up.
- **`noDataState: OK`**: with no traffic there are no errors to count, and it doesn’t alert.

The thresholds were measured with the shop healthy and a traffic script from the step (`npm run traffic`, which you’ll see in [lesson 7](/en/phase-10/incident-management-postmortem/)): not a single 5xx in 5 minutes, and a p99 of 72 ms at most (the p99 threshold is 0.5 s). The reasoning is in the file’s comments: **measure yours**, because a copied threshold alerts too much or too little.

### An email that doesn’t arrive gives no error

With the queue’s Redis frozen, paying still gives `200`: since [Phase 9](/en/phase-9/event-driven-architecture/#the-outbox-the-notice-in-the-same-transaction), it only writes to PostgreSQL. No error, and no email. That’s why step 46 adds a metric:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/src/metrics.ts" region="outbox-age" />

- **It’s read from PostgreSQL, not from Redis**, so it keeps telling the truth when what fails is Redis. With the relayer working, a few seconds; the rule alerts at 120.
- **If PostgreSQL freezes, it goes quiet**: the SDK (the library that sends the metrics) repeats the last value, lesson 5’s [flat line](/en/phase-10/prometheus-grafana-metrics/#three-types-counter-gauge-and-histogram). The errors and the slowness warn you of that.

### The fourth rule watches the others

If the API goes down, it stops sending metrics, and the first three, with no data, go quiet: **a dead API looks like a calm API.** The fourth looks at how old the last sample of the outbox age is (one arrives every 5 seconds; with the API healthy, it gave us 3.09 seconds), and alerts if it goes over 60. Unlike the others, it also alerts with no data.

### Whom it alerts, and how often

`contact.yaml` sends an email to `oncall@shop.example`, over SMTP, the email protocol, to [Mailpit](/en/phase-9/background-jobs-queues/#the-email-mailpit-and-short-waits), inside Compose’s network:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/compose.observability.yaml" region="email" />

`policy.yaml` hands out the alerts:

- each rule, in its own email (`group_by: alertname`);
- 10 seconds of waiting before the first one, in case another from the same group arrives (`group_wait`);
- up to a minute to report a change, like it being resolved (`group_interval`);
- a reminder every 4 hours if it stays the same, not every minute (`repeat_interval`).

Mailpit now keeps 5000 emails (`MP_MAX_MESSAGES`, in `compose.yaml`): with 500, the step’s traffic script deleted the alert ones in ten minutes.

### Alert fatigue

If nineteen out of twenty alerts a day ask for nothing, within a week you stop reading them, and the one that mattered gets lost. That’s <Term id="alert-fatigue">alert fatigue</Term>. It’s avoided with:

- **alerts that ask you to act now**; the rest, to a dashboard;
- **measured thresholds and a `for`**;
- **severities**: `urgent` wakes someone up, `warning` waits for working hours;
- **few reminders**, and **reviewing them**: the one that fires without anyone doing anything gets fixed or deleted.

### Failing well: the three fixes Phase 9 left

The other half is failing well: fast, clearly and without dragging the rest down. Phase 9’s final review left three places where the shop didn’t.

**1. The attempt limits waited for Redis endlessly**: 12.2 seconds in [lesson 4](/en/phase-10/opentelemetry-tracing/#freeze-redis-and-sign-in). Now each question waits **200 ms at most**, like Phase 9’s cache. At the first one that doesn’t answer, the limit considers Redis down, and only asks it again once a second, so as not to pile up commands. And meanwhile? Each limit decides:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/src/rate-limits.ts" region="shop-limits" />

- **The sign-in ones close**: `503` with `Retry-After: 5`, without waiting for Redis while it considers it down; only the once-a-second probe waits. Letting passwords through with no limit is exactly the [attack they stop](/en/phase-6/rate-limiting-secrets/#trying-nonstop-brute-force).
- **The account-creation one opens**: it lets them through and says so in the log. A few sign-ups with no limit cost less than not letting any new customer in.

**It’s a decision, not a rule**: a shop with a fake-account problem would close both.

**2. A borrowed connection that got cut could stop the API.** It’s the gap from [lesson 2](/en/phase-10/unit-integration-e2e-tests/#the-lines-no-test-runs): nobody listened to the `'error'` of a connection in use, and Node stops the process when an error has nobody listening. It happens, for example, when the [Phase 9 setting](/en/phase-9/event-driven-architecture/#a-worker-that-disappears-with-the-rows-locked) cuts a transaction that has been silent for 5 seconds. Now it’s listened to on every new connection:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/46-alerts/src/db.ts" region="borrowed-connection" />

**3. `deploy.sh` could take a copy that restarts in a loop for healthy**, if it caught it healthy in its single look. Now `wait_for_health` looks at it **twice, with about 5 seconds in between**, and the `RestartCount`, the restarts Docker has counted, has to be the same both times.

All three say the same: **when something you depend on fails, wait briefly, decide what you do and stay alive.**

### Watching from outside

In production, all this has a hole: **a server can’t warn you of its own death.** If the machine shuts down, Grafana shuts down with it. Real monitoring adds two pieces, outside the server:

1. **The metrics and the alerts, on another machine**: a service with a free plan, like Grafana Cloud (check its current limits), or the same three tools on another server. The `OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_…` addresses in the `.env` change and, if the service asks for one, a key. The rules’ queries and thresholds work the same, but there they’re loaded through the service’s API or with Terraform, not with these files, and they have to be pointed at its data source and its labels.
2. **A check from outside** (*uptime check*): a service that requests `https://your-domain/health` every minute from another network. It sees the same as a customer, and warns of what nothing inside sees: the machine off, the network cut or the certificate expired.

### On your server

`/health` doesn’t change: the healthcheck and `deploy.sh` stay the same, and each copy takes about 5 seconds longer to be considered healthy, because of the second look. The alerts don’t arrive: the server’s `.env` doesn’t have the OTLP addresses, and `compose.production.yaml` doesn’t gain Grafana. For that, [Watching from outside](#watching-from-outside).

## You have already seen it

- **The ATM that turns on its screen and says “out of service”**: alive, but not ready.
- **The smoke detector’s chirp when its battery runs low**: a device warning you that it won’t be able to warn you anymore. It’s the fourth rule.
- **A performance budget**: this course fails its build if a page goes over 400 KB of JavaScript. A limit you set yourself, like an SLO.

## Common mistakes

- **Making the liveness probe look at the database.** A PostgreSQL outage restarts every copy and fixes nothing.
- **Checking with no maximum time.** With a frozen dependency, the check waits along with it.
- **Alerting on causes** instead of what customers notice.
- **Alerting on every error.** Without a `for` or a measured threshold, a lone `500` wakes someone up.
- **Watching the server only from inside.** If the machine goes down, whoever warns you goes down with it.

## Summary

- `/health/live` says whether the process answers, without asking anyone; `/health/ready`, whether it can serve: PostgreSQL and the queue’s Redis, 500 ms each; the cache doesn’t count. `/health` stays the same.
- An SLI measures what the customer notices, the SLO gives it a target and the error budget is what’s left over: if it runs out, it’s time to fix things.
- Alerts warn of symptoms, with measured thresholds and a `for`. The shop has four: errors, p99, emails that don’t go out and an API that goes quiet.
- Failing well is waiting briefly, deciding whether you open or close, and staying alive.
- A server can’t warn you of its own death: in production, the alerts live outside.

<QuizOption text="To lower the SLO to 98% to have more room this month.">

Moving the target when it’s in the way empties it of meaning: the SLO says what your customers can expect.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="To wait, and spend those days on reliability." correct>

That’s what the budget is for: while there’s some left, you take risks; if it runs out, you fix what fails.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>

<QuizQuestion
  question="Someone proposes an urgent alert: “the API’s CPU is over 80% for 5 minutes.” What do you tell them?"
  review="Alerts on symptoms, not on causes"
>

<QuizOption text="Better to alert on the p99: high CPU that nobody notices doesn’t ask you to do anything." correct>

An alert on a symptom fires for any cause, and only when someone notices it. The CPU can go on a dashboard.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="It’s the best one: the CPU goes up before the errors, so you find out sooner.">

Sometimes it goes up before, and many other times it goes up without anyone noticing. Every alert that asks for nothing brings alert fatigue closer.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Symptom or cause doesn’t matter: any number that goes past its limit is a problem.">

It’s only a problem if someone suffers it. Alerting on everything is the fastest way to make sure nobody reads the alerts.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>

<QuizQuestion
  question="At three in the morning you get “Outgoing events stuck.” The errors panel is at zero and payments give 200. What’s going on?"
  review="An email that doesn’t arrive gives no error"
>

<QuizOption text="A false alarm: if there are no 5xx, the shop is fine.">

No errors doesn’t mean everything works. This rule exists precisely for the failure that gives no error.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="PostgreSQL is down, because the age is read from there.">

With PostgreSQL down, payments would fail, and the age would stand still: this rule would go quiet.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Payments work, but emails don’t go out: look at the queue." correct>

The payment only writes to PostgreSQL; the email needs the queue’s Redis and the worker. Look at those two.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>

<QuizQuestion
  question="Your shop limits attempts to redeem a discount coupon to 5 per hour, so nobody can guess codes. If Redis doesn’t answer, do you open or close that limit?"
  review="Failing well: the three fixes Phase 9 left"
>

<QuizOption text="Open: a limit should never stop someone from buying.">

Open, anyone can try codes with no limit while the outage lasts, exactly what the limit stops.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Closed: open would let people try codes with no limit." correct>

Like signing in: the limit protects against whoever tries blindly. Without a coupon you can still buy, at the normal price.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="It doesn’t matter: with Redis down, nothing in the shop works.">

With Redis down, paying still works: only what needs it fails, and what you decide to close.

</QuizOption>
