## The problem

Imagine it’s three in the morning and you get two emails in a row: “5xx errors on /v2” and “Outgoing events stuck.” The alerts from [lesson 6](/en/phase-10/health-checks-alerts/) have done their job: you found out before your customers. And now what?

What your gut asks for is to open the code and look for the bug, while customers still can’t get in. Or to restart everything, and wipe out the clues. And in the morning nobody remembers when anything happened.

**Handling a failure in production has an order**, and it’s better learned on a quiet Tuesday. This lesson closes the phase with a practice <Term id="incident">incident</Term> in your shop, from the first alert to the final report.

## The analogy

<Analogy>

A pipe bursts at home and water runs down the hallway. First you close the main valve, even if you don’t know which pipe it is. Then the plumber finds the break and replaces it.

And afterwards you think: do I replace the old pipes? Do I put in a leak detector? Nobody blames whoever turned on the tap: it would have burst anyway.

<div slot="limits">

- Closing the valve stops the damage. On a server, mitigating doesn’t undo everything: the delayed emails arrive, but late.
- You can see water. On a server you only see what you measure.
- A pipe bursts for one reason. An incident almost never has just one.

</div>

</Analogy>

Closing the valve is **mitigating**; the plumber, **fixing**; thinking about the old pipes, **learning**.

## How it really works

### What an incident is

An incident is a failure customers notice now and that asks for action now. A misaligned button gets fixed on Monday. Nobody being able to sign in is an incident.

**Saying it out loud changes how you work**: one person leads, you talk in a single place, you write down the time of everything and nobody touches anything without saying so.

### Detect, mitigate, fix, learn

Every incident goes through four phases:

| Phase | The question | In the practice incident |
|---|---|---|
| Detect | Is something wrong, and how badly? | Two alert emails, at about two and a half minutes |
| Mitigate | How do I stop the damage now? | Unfreeze the queue’s Redis |
| Fix | Why did it happen, and how do I keep it from coming back? | Find out why it froze, and change that |
| Learn | What do we change for next time? | The postmortem, with its actions |

### Mitigate first, investigate later

While you investigate, customers keep suffering. **First you stop the damage, even if you don’t know the cause**:

- **If it started right after a deployment, you roll back.** It’s Phase 8’s <Term id="rollback">rollback</Term> ([roll back on purpose](/en/phase-8/ci-cd-github-actions/#roll-back-on-purpose)): a few seconds, and then you investigate calmly. It brings back the code, [not the schema](/en/phase-8/ci-cd-github-actions/#a-rollback-brings-back-the-code-not-the-schema).
- **If a piece has hung, you restart it**, or replace it with another.
- **If a new part fails, you switch it off**, and the rest of the shop keeps going.

Before restarting, **save the clues**: Jaeger keeps the traces in memory, and recreating a container takes its logs with it. `docker compose logs api > incident-api.log` takes a second.

### Roles: who leads, who fixes, who communicates

In a team, a serious incident hands out three roles:

- **Whoever leads** (*incident commander*): decides what gets tried and in what order, and doesn’t type: they see the whole picture.
- **Whoever fixes**: looks at dashboards, traces and logs, and runs what’s decided.
- **Whoever communicates**: tells support and customers, and writes down the time of each thing.

**If you’re alone, all three roles are yours.** Write down in a file the time of everything you see or do: it will be the postmortem’s timeline. Tell people early, even if it’s just two lines. And every ten minutes, look up: is what you’re trying bringing you closer to the solution?

### Telling it while it lasts: the status page

A **status page** tells your customers what’s failing, what isn’t and when you’ll give more news. An example, GitHub’s: [githubstatus.com](https://www.githubstatus.com/). Three sentences are enough: “You can’t sign in. Orders and payments work. We’ll update at 3:30.”

It lives **outside your server**, like [watching from outside](/en/phase-10/health-checks-alerts/#watching-from-outside): if the machine goes down, the page is still there to say so.

### The blameless postmortem

When everything works again, you write a <Term id="postmortem">postmortem</Term>: what happened, when, why and what changes. **Blameless**: it doesn’t ask who made a mistake, but why the system allowed it. If one person could break production with one command, the next one would do the same.

A real case: in 2017, a person at GitLab deleted the main database by mistake, thinking they were on the secondary one. Its [public postmortem](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/2017/02/10/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/) doesn’t stop at the person: with a chain of “why?”, it gets to the backups failing without anyone knowing, because the emails warning about it were being rejected.

### Being on call, and what it costs

Someone has to read the alerts at three in the morning: that’s being <Term id="on-call">on call</Term>. It costs sleep and plans, and a badly run rotation burns people out. It becomes bearable like this:

- **In shifts**, like pharmacies: never the same person all the time.
- **Only what’s urgent wakes you up.** It’s the [alerts’ severity](/en/phase-10/health-checks-alerts/#alert-fatigue): `urgent` wakes you up, `warning` waits until morning.
- **Every alert says what to look at**, like “Look at the worker and the queue’s Redis.” With a page of steps per alert (a *runbook*), anyone on the shift can handle it.
- **It gets counted**: how many times it woke someone up, and how many for no reason. And it gets paid, or compensated with time off.
- **With someone as backup**, in case whoever is on call doesn’t answer.

If you run your shop alone, being on call is yours: decide what wakes you up.

### Diagnose: the trace

In Jaeger, `http://localhost:16686`, paste the log’s `trace_id` in **Lookup by Trace ID**, as in [lesson 4](/en/phase-10/opentelemetry-tracing/#from-the-log-to-the-trace). We looked ours up right away, and it wasn’t there yet: the API sends its spans in batches, every 5 seconds or so. If that happens to you, wait a few seconds. One from a few seconds earlier, summarized as there:

```text
POST /v2/session                                          shop-api              205.34 ms   +0 ms   error
```

**A single span, with an error, and none from Redis.** Redis was asked: a span is sent to Jaeger when it ends, and the commands to Redis were still waiting for an answer.

To see them all, in **Search**: service `shop-api`, **Span Name** `POST /v2/session`, **Attributes** `http.response.status_code=503` and **Min Duration** `100ms`. We got the 20 it shows at once, every one with a single span, and in the log, **the 131 took between 200 and 210 ms**. The downed limit only lets one question through to Redis per second, and Carmen signs in every 1.3 seconds: every sign-in was that question, and waited its 200 ms. With more customers, the others would get the `503` instantly.

### Mitigate

In a real incident, mitigating would be restarting that Redis, or swapping it for another. Here, unfreezing it is enough:

<TryIt
  title="12. Redis, back"
  cmd="docker compose unpause redis"
  output={` Container shop-redis-1 Unpaused `}
>

We did it at 20:53:22 (18:53:22 in UTC): **27 seconds after the first email**. In the second terminal, the next sign-in already gives `200`.

</TryIt>

Go back to Jaeger and open the same trace. Now it has its Redis spans:

```text
POST /v2/session                                          shop-api              205.34 ms   +0 ms   error
  redis-ZREMRANGEBYSCORE                                  shop-api            19073.02 ms   +0 ms
  redis-ZRANGE                                            shop-api            19073.00 ms   +0 ms
```

**The request answered at 205 ms, but its commands to Redis lasted 19 seconds**: from 18:53:04 until the `unpause`. The request stopped waiting; the command kept waiting in the Redis client. The first sign-in of the incident, at 18:50:28, has spans of 174,816 ms, also until the `unpause`. **It’s lesson 6’s cutoff, seen from the inside**: without it, every sign-in would have waited that long.

### Write the postmortem

Copy the template into a folder for your incidents:

```sh
mkdir -p incidents
cp docs/postmortem.md incidents/2026-10-24-frozen-redis.md
```

Fill it in with your times and numbers, and delete its comments. Ours, in UTC:

### Our postmortem

**The queue’s Redis, frozen.** October 24, 2026. Duration: 2 min 55 s, from 18:50:27 to 18:53:22; detected at 2 min 28 s. Severity: urgent. Author: whoever was on call. Status: draft.

**Summary.** The queue’s Redis stopped answering for three minutes. Sign-ins failed with `503` and confirmation emails didn’t go out; orders and payments worked. When it was unfrozen, everything came back within a second.

**Impact.**

- 131 failed sign-ins, in about 200 ms each.
- 131 emails delayed by up to almost three minutes; none lost (283 payments, 283 emails).
- 131 5xx responses: with lesson 6’s SLO and a million requests a month, 1.3% of the error budget.

**Timeline** (UTC):

| Time | What happened | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 18:50:27 | The queue’s Redis frozen; the first `session 503` a second later | Terminal, `npm run traffic` |
| 18:52:40 | “Outgoing events stuck,” fired | The alert’s email |
| 18:52:50 | “5xx errors on /v2,” fired | The alert’s email |
| 18:52:55 | First alert email | Mailpit |
| 18:53:07 | Diagnosis: only `/v2/session` fails; limits and relayer without an answer from Redis | Dashboard and log |
| 18:53:22 | Mitigated: `docker compose unpause redis` | Terminal |
| 18:53:23 | The 131 held emails arrive; the next sign-in gives `200` | Mailpit, `npm run traffic` |
| 18:55:02 | The last alert resolved, the errors one | Mailpit |

**Cause.** The queue’s Redis stopped answering (in this practice, on purpose). Why did sign-ins fail? Because their limits live in that Redis and close without it. Why didn’t the emails go out? Because the relayer couldn’t enqueue them. Why did everything fail at once? Because limits and queue share a single Redis, with no copy.

**What went well.** Payments kept going because they only write to PostgreSQL, and the emails waited in the outbox until the queue came back. The limits answered in 200 ms, not in 12 seconds. The alerts arrived with their hint (“Look at the worker and the queue’s Redis”). No email was lost.

**What went wrong.** Detecting it took two and a half minutes. The trace didn’t show the Redis spans until it came back. The queue metric said `0` while nothing went out, and the outgoing events’ age isn’t on the dashboard.

**Where we were lucky.** There was a single customer buying. Redis was frozen, not down: it came back with its data.

**Actions.**

| Action | Owner | Due | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| The oldest outgoing event’s age, on “The shop” dashboard | Whoever is on call | October 31 | Pending |
| A page per alert, with the steps, linked from its description | Whoever is on call | November 7 | Pending |

### On your server

This lesson doesn’t bring a step. On your server there are no alerts (lesson 6). What you do take with you is the `incidents/` folder, in the repository.

## You have already seen it

- **Pharmacies on duty at night**: in shifts, so no single one opens every night.
- **The status page** of a service you use, when it fails: “We’re investigating problems with…”
- **Aviation accident reports**: by international rule, they look for what to change, not whom to blame. That’s where the blameless postmortem comes from.

## Common mistakes

- **Investigating before mitigating.** While you look for the cause, customers still can’t get in.
- **Restarting everything without saving anything.** The clues go away with the restart.
- **Looking for someone to blame.** Next time nobody will tell anything, and the flaw will still be there.
- **Actions without an owner or a date.** “Be more careful” changes nothing.
- **Being on call, always the same person.** They burn out, and without them nobody knows what to do.

## Summary

- An incident is a failure your customers notice now. It’s handled in order: detect, mitigate, fix, learn.
- First you stop the damage (roll back, restart, switch off), saving the clues; the cause is looked for later.
- Whoever leads, whoever fixes and whoever communicates: in a team, three people; alone, you, with a timeline and early updates.
- The blameless postmortem asks why the system allowed it, and ends in actions with an owner and a date.
- Being on call costs: in shifts, only what’s urgent wakes you up, and every alert says what to look at.

<QuizOption text="Asking why one command could break production." correct>

It stops at a person. What gets changed is that the system allows it. And “be more careful” isn’t an action.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Nothing: it tells the truth, and the action has an owner.">

It’s true, but useless: the next person will break the same thing, and nobody will want to tell their mistakes.

</QuizOption>

Without the name, it still blames someone. Blameless is changing the question: what allowed the failure, not who caused it.

<QuizQuestion
  question="Which of these is a good action for a postmortem?"

>

<QuizOption text="The outgoing event’s age, on the dashboard: Ana, by the 31st." correct>

Concrete, checkable, with an owner and a date: in a month you know whether it’s done.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Watch Redis better from now on, all of us.">

“Better” can’t be checked, and what belongs to everyone belongs to no one: nobody will know when it’s done.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Make sure the queue’s Redis never freezes again.">

It’s a wish, not a change. The action is what you’ll do so it doesn’t happen, or to find out sooner if it does.
