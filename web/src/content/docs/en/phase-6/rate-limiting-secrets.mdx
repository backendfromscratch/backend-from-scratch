## The problem

Imagine a Sunday at three in the morning. A program sends Ana's email, the administrator's, to `POST /v2/session`, each time with a password from a list of the most used ones. Each attempt takes about 30 ms, which is what the slow hash from [lesson 1, “Passwords,”](/en/phase-6/how-to-store-passwords/#slow-on-purpose-argon2id-bcrypt-and-scrypt) costs: the first 1000, in half a minute. Nothing in the shop asks it to stop.

At the same time, another program crawls GitHub looking for keys uploaded by mistake. If it finds the shop's `WEBHOOK_SECRET` in an old commit, it doesn't guess anything: it signs fake payment notifications, and the shop believes them ([webhooks lesson](/en/phase-4/webhooks-idempotency/)).

This lesson closes both doors: **a limit on how many times something can be tried, and secrets that never leave the server.**

## The analogy

<Analogy>

If you get your phone's PIN wrong several times in a row, it stops asking you for a while: “Try again in 1 minute.” To you it costs a minute, once. To someone who finds it on a park bench and wants to try all 10,000 possible PINs, an eternity.

<div slot="limits">

- The phone knows whoever is typing is holding it. The shop only sees an email and an IP: it can't tell Ana from someone pretending to be her, so whoever tries with her email locks her out too.
- The phone can only be reached by hand. The shop, from the whole internet, and an attacker can spread their attempts across thousands of addresses.

</div>

</Analogy>

## How it really works

### Trying nonstop: brute force

What happens at three in the morning is **<Term id="brute-force">brute force</Term>**: trying possibilities one after another until one is right. Against a login it takes three forms:

- **Many passwords against one account:** Ana's.
- **One password against many accounts** (*password spraying*): `123456` with every known email.
- **The email and password pairs from another leak:** the [*credential stuffing* from lesson 1](/en/phase-6/how-to-store-passwords/#storing-it-as-it-is-one-leak-shows-them-all).

Lesson 1 made each attempt against a stolen table expensive, but here nobody needs the table: asking the server is enough. The brake is **<Term id="rate-limiting">rate limiting</Term>**: a maximum number of attempts per period of time. With 10 every 15 minutes, the 1000 passwords go from half a minute to more than a day, and whoever watches the logs sees it coming.

It also protects the server: each attempt costs it about 30 ms of processor and 19 MiB of memory, and thousands at once would leave it no time for anyone else.

### Three ways to count attempts

With the shop's limit, 10 attempts every 15 minutes:

| | Fixed window | Sliding window | Token bucket |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it counts | One counter per clock slot (from 10:00 to 10:15, from 10:15 to 10:30…), which goes back to zero when each one starts | The attempts of the last 15 minutes, counted from right now | 10 tokens: each attempt spends one, and one comes back every 90 seconds |
| What it stores | A number | The time of each attempt | The tokens and when the last one came back |
| Its drawback | Between two slots it lets twice as many through: 10 at 10:14 and 10 at 10:15 | It stores more data | After spending all 10, tokens keep coming back: almost 20 fit in 15 minutes |
| Where you see it | Quotas: “1000 requests per day” | Sign-ins | APIs that accept short bursts, like Amazon API Gateway |

### The shop counts with a sliding window

The shop writes down the time of each attempt and, to decide, counts the ones in the last 15 minutes (*sliding window log*). It's the most exact, and here storing them costs little: at most, 10 instants per email and 30 per IP.

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/31-rate-limits/src/rate-limits.ts" region="check" />

- **`store.attempts(key(of), now - window)`**: the attempts of that key (the limit and the email, or the limit and the IP) in the last 15 minutes.
- **`allowed`**: another one fits if there are fewer than `max`.
- **`retryIn`**: in how many seconds the attempt that's in the way leaves the window. Then one fits again.
- **`check` only looks.** `record`, next to it, stores an attempt with the current time.

### By email and by IP: the shop's limits

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/31-rate-limits/src/rate-limits.ts" region="shop-limits" />

- **`signInByEmail`**: 10 failures per email. It protects each account even if the attempts come from a thousand IPs.
- **`signInByIp`**: 30 failures per IP, whatever the email. It slows down whoever tries many accounts from one place.
- **`accountsByIp`**: 5 new accounts per IP and hour, whether they go well or badly. It slows down mass sign-ups, and whoever uses the [`409` from lesson 1](/en/phase-6/how-to-store-passwords/#the-users-table-and-post-v2accounts) to ask which emails have an account.

When signing in, only the failures count. The route, in `src/v2.ts`:

- **The limits, before the password.** `checkSignInLimits` looks at both; if the email or the IP reached the maximum, `tooManyAttempts` answers without computing any hash. **Not even the right password gets in:** if it did, whoever is trying would keep trying, and a `200` would tell them they got it.

### 429 and Retry-After: how long to wait

When nothing else fits, the shop answers `429 Too Many Requests`, a 4xx <Term id="status-code">status code</Term> from [Phase 2](/en/phase-2/http-status-codes/), with <Term id="problem-details">Problem Details</Term> and three <Term id="header">headers</Term>:

| Header | Example | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| `Retry-After` | `870` | How many seconds to wait |
| `RateLimit-Policy` | `"session-email";q=10;w=900` | The rule: at most `q` attempts in `w` seconds |
| `RateLimit` | `"session-email";r=0;t=870` | You have `r` left, and in `t` seconds the oldest one leaves |

`Retry-After` is standard (RFC 9110). **`RateLimit-Policy` and `RateLimit` are an IETF draft, not a standard:** their shape has changed between versions and may change again. Many APIs use their own, like `x-ratelimit-remaining` in GitHub's. The shop also sends `RateLimit` with every `400`, so a program knows how much it has left.

### The traps: shared IPs and locked-out victims

**An IP isn't a person.** Because of the <Term id="nat">NAT</Term> from [Phase 0](/en/phase-0/ip-ports-sockets/#how-your-private-ip-address-gets-out-to-the-internet-nat), an office, a university or a carrier's phones go out to the internet with a single <Term id="public-ip">public IP address</Term>. With 10 failures per IP, a few absent-minded people would lock out the whole campus: that's why the IP's is 30. And whoever attacks from thousands of borrowed IPs doesn't reach the limit of any of them: that's what the email's is for.

The limit by email has its own trap: **anyone can use up another person's attempts.** With ten made-up passwords, someone leaves Ana unable to get in for 15 minutes. That's why it lasts a while and isn't a permanent lock. Big sites add a CAPTCHA, or an email to the owner.

Against *credential stuffing* spread across thousands of IPs, what really works is the [second factor from lesson 2, “Sessions”](/en/phase-6/sessions-and-cookies/#a-second-factor-when-the-password-is-not-enough).

### Secrets, out of the code and out of Git

A <Term id="secret">secret</Term> never goes in the code, as you saw in the [environment variables lesson](/en/phase-1/environment-variables/#keeping-secrets-out-of-the-code): it goes in the `.env`, and the `.env`, in `.gitignore`.

What people forget is that **Git doesn't forget**. Each commit is a snapshot of the project ([“Git internals”](/en/phase-1/git-internals/#a-commit-is-a-snapshot-with-a-fingerprint)), and deleting the secret in the next commit doesn't remove it from the earlier ones. **A secret that reached Git has leaked, and it gets changed.** Cleaning the history comes later, and it doesn't delete the copies already made.

### No example secrets: the server won't start

Another way to leak a secret: not changing the example one, which is in Git for anyone to see. From this lesson on, `src/config.ts` doesn't allow it:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/31-rate-limits/src/config.ts" region="secret" />

- **`.min(32, …)`**: at least 32 characters. A short one is guessed by brute force, and with no limits: whoever has a signed notification tries secrets on their own computer until the signature matches.
- **`.refine(…)`**: a check of our own in [Zod](/en/phase-3/zod-validation/), which rejects the ones starting with `change-this`, the ones in `.env.example`.
- **`secret('WEBHOOK_SECRET')` and `secret('OIDC_CLIENT_SECRET')`**: the same rules for both.

**Better not to start than to start with a secret that can be guessed.**

### Generating a secret and rotating it

`openssl rand -base64 32` takes 32 bytes from the system's random generator and writes them in base64: 44 characters, 256 bits. No computer in the world guesses them by trying.

**Rotating** a secret is replacing it with a new one: as soon as it leaks or may have leaked (in Git, in a chat, in a log), when someone who knew it leaves, and every so often if your rules ask for it, so you know how to do it the day it's urgent.

Each secret has two sides, and both get changed: the `WEBHOOK_SECRET`, in the shop and at the gateway; the `OIDC_CLIENT_SECRET`, in the shop and at the provider; the database password, in PostgreSQL and in the `DATABASE_URL`. That's why many gateways let the old one stay valid for a few hours.

### GitHub looks for secrets pushed by mistake

GitHub checks what gets pushed, looking for keys with known formats, from hundreds of services (Amazon, Stripe, GitHub…). It's *secret scanning*. If it finds one, it warns the repository's owner and, in public repositories, the key's service, which can revoke it. With *push protection*, it rejects the `git push` before it arrives.

This course's repository has both turned on, free because it's public. But a secret that came out of `openssl` has no format GitHub recognizes: **the defense is that it never reaches Git.**

### The database, with just the permissions it needs

The `DATABASE_URL` carries the password of the `shop` role. Whoever has it can do whatever that role can do:

`\dt` lists the tables, and `Owner` says whose each one is: all of them belong to `shop`, which is also the database's owner since the [introduction to Phase 5](/en/phase-5/#the-shop-role). If `notifications` is missing, you didn't apply the migration from lesson 7.

</TryIt>

Being the owner lets it do everything inside `shop`: read the hashes in `users`, change orders, `DROP TABLE orders` or delete the whole database. It isn't a superuser: it doesn't create roles, or read other roles' tables, or the server's files. It's <Term id="least-privilege">least privilege</Term> halfway.

It has to be the owner because of the <Term id="migration">migrations</Term>, which create tables. A stricter setup uses `shop` only to migrate when deploying, and another role for the API, which only reads and writes rows:

```sql
-- As a superuser, connected to the shop database
CREATE ROLE shop_app LOGIN PASSWORD '…';
GRANT USAGE ON SCHEMA public TO shop_app;
GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON ALL TABLES IN SCHEMA public TO shop_app;
ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES FOR ROLE shop IN SCHEMA public
  GRANT SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE ON TABLES TO shop_app;
```

`GRANT USAGE` opens the schema, the folder of the tables; the second `GRANT`, the tables that already exist; `ALTER DEFAULT PRIVILEGES`, the ones the migrations will create. Tested on PostgreSQL 18: as `shop_app`, `DROP TABLE orders` answers `ERROR:  must be owner of table orders`. It can still delete rows: least privilege reduces the damage, it doesn't remove it. The project uses a single role so as not to complicate the `.env`.

## You have already seen it

- **“Too many attempts. Try again later,”** or a sudden CAPTCHA, when you get it wrong several times signing in to a website.
- **The card the ATM keeps after three wrong PINs:** a lock with no end date, which the bank can afford because it can see you at the branch.
- **APIs with limits,** like the AI model APIs from Phase 11, which answer `429`.

## Common mistakes

- **Locking the account forever,** or letting people in with the right password during the lock.
- **Limiting by the connection's IP** behind a *proxy* ([Phase 8](/en/phase-8/nginx-reverse-proxy/)): everything comes from it. And `X-Forwarded-For`, the header with the real IP, is only valid if your *proxy* sets it: anyone can write it.
- **Counting in each server's memory, with several servers.**
- **Deleting a secret pushed to Git and still using it.**
- **Leaving the `.env.example` secret,** or a short one.
- **Connecting the API with the database's superuser.**

## Summary

- A limit on attempts per period of time makes brute force slow and visible.
- The fixed window lets twice as many through between slots; the token bucket accepts bursts; the sliding window, the shop's, counts exactly the last 15 minutes.
- When over the limit, `429` with `Retry-After`; `RateLimit-Policy` and `RateLimit` are a draft.
- By email and by IP, with their traps: shared IPs and locked-out victims. Keeping them in memory works for one server; with several, Redis.
- Long, random secrets, never in Git, rotated when they leak; and the database, with just the permissions it needs.

<QuizOption text="Yes: GitHub only shows the last commit, and that one no longer has it.">

`.gitignore` only makes Git not see the file from now on.

<QuizOption text="Read and write rows, but not drop tables: that requires being a superuser.">

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="The same as the API: PostgreSQL only allows what the shop’s code does.">

PostgreSQL doesn't know what your code does: it lets the role do what its permissions allow, wherever it connects from.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Everything inside the shop database, even dropping tables, but not creating roles." correct>

With a separate role for the API, one that wasn't the owner, the damage would stay in the rows.
