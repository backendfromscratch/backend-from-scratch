## The problem

Imagine a bookstore chain wants to sell Ana and Luis's products through their API. Before signing, it sends them a security questionnaire that starts like this: “How does your API cover the risks of the OWASP Top 10?”

Ana has eight lessons of defenses: hashed passwords, sessions, roles, parameters, limits… But she added them one by one, as each problem came up. Is a whole family of flaws missing? And how does she explain it to someone who hasn't read her code?

**She needs a map: a short list of the flaws that come up most often, with names everyone knows.**

## The analogy

<Analogy>

A car's safety inspection, which many countries require, doesn't check everything: it looks at the points that cause the most accidents, like the brakes and the lights. The list comes from years of data, and in any garage “failed on brakes” means the same thing.

The OWASP Top 10 is that list for web applications: the ten most serious kinds of flaw, each with a name everyone recognizes.

<div slot="limits">

- You pass or fail the inspection. The Top 10 isn't a certificate: it's a list to raise awareness. To check point by point, OWASP has another guide, the ASVS.
- The car doesn't change between two inspections. Your code changes every week: the review never ends.

</div>

</Analogy>

## How it really works

### OWASP: a foundation that shares what it knows

<Term id="owasp">OWASP</Term> is an open application security community, backed by a nonprofit foundation. Almost all the work is done by volunteers, it doesn't depend on any company and everything it publishes is free.

You already know it: its short guides, the *cheat sheets*, are in the “Further reading” of almost every lesson in this phase, like the one on passwords in [lesson 1](/en/phase-6/how-to-store-passwords/).

Its best-known document is the **Top 10**, the ten most serious risks for web applications; the 2025 edition is the eighth. Each risk groups many similar flaws. Almost all of them come from data on tests of real applications; two come from a survey of professionals, because tests barely detect them.

### The Top 10:2025, one by one

The list, taken from [the Top 10 website](https://top10.owasp.org/2025/) on October 14, 2026:

| Risk | In one sentence | Where you've seen it |
|---|---|---|
| **A01 Broken Access Control** | Someone sees or does what isn't theirs to see or do: another customer's order, an admin route. | Roles and BOLA in [lesson 5](/en/phase-6/rbac-roles-permissions/); <Term id="csrf">CSRF</Term> and <Term id="ssrf">SSRF</Term> in [lesson 7](/en/phase-6/csrf-ssrf/) |
| **A02 Security Misconfiguration** | The code is fine, but the configuration isn't: missing headers, sample accounts, cookies without `HttpOnly`. | The cookie's attributes in [lesson 2](/en/phase-6/sessions-and-cookies/); the sample secrets refused in [lesson 8](/en/phase-6/rate-limiting-secrets/); this lesson's headers |
| **A03 Software Supply Chain Failures** | The flaw comes in with other people's code: a package with a known flaw, or a poisoned one. | The [`.npmrc` from Phase 3](/en/phase-3/what-is-node-js/#the-npmrc-three-lines-against-the-2025-attacks); `npm audit` in this lesson |
| **A04 Cryptographic Failures** | Data that travels or is stored without the protection it needs. | <Term id="tls">TLS</Term> in [Phase 0](/en/phase-0/tls-and-https/); argon2id in [lesson 1](/en/phase-6/how-to-store-passwords/) |
| **A05 Injection** | A piece of data that the database or the browser ends up running as code. | Parameters in [Phase 5](/en/phase-5/node-postgres/); <Term id="sql-injection">SQL injection</Term> and <Term id="xss">XSS</Term> in [lesson 6](/en/phase-6/sql-injection-xss/) |
| **A06 Insecure Design** | No single line fails: a defense nobody thought of is missing. | The shared `ADMIN_TOKEN` from the [introduction](/en/phase-6/) |
| **A07 Authentication Failures** | The shop lets in someone who isn't who they say: weak passwords, unlimited attempts, sessions that never expire. | Lessons 1 to 4, and the limits in lesson 8 |
| **A08 Software or Data Integrity Failures** | Code or data is taken as good without checking that nobody has changed it. | The hashes in the [lockfile](/en/phase-3/what-is-node-js/#node_modules-and-the-lockfile) (Phase 3); the signature of the <Term id="webhook">webhooks</Term> ([Phase 4](/en/phase-4/webhooks-idempotency/)) and of the <Term id="jwt">JWT</Term> ([lesson 3](/en/phase-6/what-is-jwt/)) |
| **A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures** | An attack happens and nobody finds out: it isn't in the <Term id="log">log</Term>, or nobody looks at it. | The [logs from Phase 3](/en/phase-3/node-errors-and-logging/); this lesson |
| **A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions** | An unexpected error leaves the program in a strange state: it crashes, shows too much or lets someone through. | The [errors from Phase 3](/en/phase-3/node-errors-and-logging/#a-500-that-gives-nothing-away); the [transactions](/en/phase-5/acid-transactions/) from Phase 5 |

CSRF and SSRF don't have a row of their own: they're inside A01, because they're requests made with someone else's power, your session or the server's network. Compared with 2021, A02 climbs from fifth place to second, A03 widens “vulnerable components” to the whole supply chain, and A10 is new.

### A03: the supply chain, in this course

In [Phase 3](/en/phase-3/what-is-node-js/) you saw the 2025 attacks on npm and the project's `.npmrc`: a 7-day wait for every version, no commands run when installing, and exact versions. This site's repository does something similar with pnpm, and on top of that Dependabot, a GitHub robot, proposes the new versions every week, with the same wait except for security fixes.

What's still missing is knowing whether a version you already have has a known flaw: `npm audit` tells you that, in “Try it.”

### A06 and A10: what no package fixes

**Insecure design isn't fixed by programming better.** The `ADMIN_TOKEN` from the introduction didn't have a single code flaw, and yet Ana, Luis and the delivery company shared one key. The defense is to look at the API the way an attacker would before programming it.

**In the face of an error, fail closed:** if something goes wrong, the answer is “no.”

- **Without a valid secret, the server doesn't start** ([Phase 3](/en/phase-3/node-errors-and-logging/#the-configuration-is-checked-at-startup)), and since [lesson 8](/en/phase-6/rate-limiting-secrets/), not with the one in `.env.example` either.
- **A `500` gives nothing away about the inside** ([Phase 3](/en/phase-3/node-errors-and-logging/#a-500-that-gives-nothing-away)): an error that shows too much is part of A10.
- **A transaction that fails leaves nothing half-done** ([Phase 5](/en/phase-5/acid-transactions/#if-one-command-fails-the-whole-transaction-fails)).
- **With too many failures, the `429` comes before the password is even looked at** (lesson 8).

### The API Top 10 (2023)

OWASP has another list just for APIs, the [API Security Top 10](https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0x11-t10/), from 2023:

| Risk | In the shop |
|---|---|
| **API1 Broken Object Level Authorization** | <Term id="bola">BOLA</Term>: whose each order is (lesson 5) |
| **API2 Broken Authentication** | Passwords, sessions, tokens and keys (lessons 1 to 4) |
| **API3 Broken Object Property Level Authorization** | The sign-up ignores an extra `role` (in “Try it”) |
| **API4 Unrestricted Resource Consumption** | Limits on attempts (lesson 8) and on page size ([Phase 4](/en/phase-4/api-pagination/)) |
| **API5 Broken Function Level Authorization** | Every route, with its `requirePermission` (lesson 5) |
| **API6 Unrestricted Access to Sensitive Business Flows** | 5 sign-ups per IP per hour (lesson 8) |
| **API7 Server Side Request Forgery** | The delivery notification URL, checked (lesson 7) |
| **API8 Security Misconfiguration** | The headers and CORS ([Phase 2](/en/phase-2/what-is-cors/)) |
| **API9 Improper Inventory Management** | `/v1`, deprecated, was closed just like `/v2` (lesson 5) |
| **API10 Unsafe Consumption of APIs** | The webhook's signature and the provider's `id_token`, checked |

### Logs: the event, yes; the secret, never

In [Phase 3](/en/phase-3/node-errors-and-logging/#structured-logs-one-json-line-per-event), the shop started writing one line per request. It still does, in `src/middleware/request-log.ts`:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/31-rate-limits/src/middleware/request-log.ts" region="request-log" />

- **Neither the body nor the headers:** the password, the cookie and `Authorization` never reach the log.
- **`c.req.path` is the path without the <Term id="query-string">*query string*</Term>:** so the OAuth `code`, or a token someone puts in the URL, doesn't get there either.
- **pino writes JSON:** a line break inside a value stays in its text, and nobody can slip a fake line into the log.

A09 asks for more: **record the security events** (sign-ins, denied permissions, role changes) with what's needed to investigate them. According to [OWASP's logging guide](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Logging_Cheat_Sheet.html):

| Recorded | Never recorded |
|---|---|
| What happened (`failed sign-in`), when, and the request id | The password, not even the wrong one |
| Which account (its id, or the email tried) | The session id or the cookie |
| From which IP | Tokens, API keys and the `Authorization` header |
| The result: `400`, `403`, `429` | `DATABASE_URL` and any other <Term id="secret">secret</Term> |

And A09 is called *Logging and Alerting*: a log nobody reads doesn't warn anyone of anything. Alerts come in [Phase 10](/en/phase-10/health-checks-alerts/).

### The headers, before and after

To compare, start the shop from lesson 5, from before `secureHeaders()`, next to yours. If you already have the `28-roles` folder, skip `curl` and `tar`:


You already saw `x-content-type-options: nosniff` and `referrer-policy` in [Phase 2](/en/phase-2/http-headers/#security-headers), and `x-xss-protection: 0` in lesson 6. Of the others, the ones that matter most:

| Header | What it asks the browser for |
|---|---|
| `strict-transport-security` | To come in only over HTTPS for 180 days. Over `http://localhost` the browser ignores it: it will act once there's HTTPS, in [Phase 8](/en/phase-8/lets-encrypt-nginx/) |
| `x-frame-options: SAMEORIGIN` | That another site doesn't put your pages inside its own, with an invisible button on top |
| `cross-origin-opener-policy` and `cross-origin-resource-policy` | To isolate your pages and responses from those of other sites |

The rest adjust minor details. `content-security-policy` is missing on purpose: it goes only on `/shop` (lesson 6). Stop the shop from lesson 5 with <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>C</kbd> and go back to your project with `cd ../shop-api`.

### npm audit: packages with known vulnerabilities

`npm audit` compares the versions in the lockfile with a public database of known flaws, GitHub's, and tells you which ones affect you.

<TryIt
  title="3. Your packages’ warnings"
  cmd="npm audit"
  output={`# npm audit report

esbuild  <=0.24.2
Severity: moderate
esbuild enables any website to send any requests to the development server and read the response - https://github.com/advisories/GHSA-67mh-4wv8-2f99
fix available via \`npm audit fix --force\`
Will install drizzle-kit@0.18.1, which is a breaking change
node_modules/@esbuild-kit/core-utils/node_modules/esbuild
  @esbuild-kit/core-utils  *
  Depends on vulnerable versions of esbuild
  node_modules/@esbuild-kit/core-utils
    @esbuild-kit/esm-loader  *
    Depends on vulnerable versions of @esbuild-kit/core-utils
    node_modules/@esbuild-kit/esm-loader
      drizzle-kit  0.19.0 - 1.0.0-beta.1-fd8bfcc
      Depends on vulnerable versions of @esbuild-kit/esm-loader
      node_modules/drizzle-kit

4 moderate severity vulnerabilities

To address all issues (including breaking changes), run:
  npm audit fix --force`}
>

It's the warning you see when installing since [lesson 9 of Phase 5](/en/phase-5/orm-drizzle-prisma/). From top to bottom:

- **The package and the affected versions:** `esbuild`, up to 0.24.2.
- **The severity:** `low`, `moderate`, `high` or `critical`. This one is moderate.
- **The flaw:** another site could read the responses of esbuild's development server, which drizzle-kit doesn't start.
- **The chain:** it comes from `drizzle-kit` through two packages in between; the four count as four warnings.
- **The proposed fix:** `--force` would install drizzle-kit 0.18.1, old and incompatible. **Don't run it:** it trades a moderate warning for a broken project.

</TryIt>

<TryIt
  title="4. Only what the server uses"
  cmd="npm audit --omit=dev"
  output={`found 0 vulnerabilities`}
>

`--omit=dev` leaves out the `devDependencies`: drizzle-kit only generates migrations on your computer. The server has no known flaw.

</TryIt>

So what now? Today, nothing: on October 14, 2026, the 0.31.11 you have is drizzle-kit's latest stable version. The 1.0 versions, still in testing, no longer include that esbuild: the fix will arrive with the stable 1.0, after its 7-day wait. If one day a `high` or a `critical` shows up in a package the server uses, you update it to the version that fixes it; if that version is less than 7 days old, you skip the wait just for it, on purpose.

A development package isn't harmless: when you use it, it runs on your computer with your permissions, exactly where [Shai-Hulud](/en/phase-3/what-is-node-js/#the-npmrc-three-lines-against-the-2025-attacks) went looking for keys. That's why you read every warning: what flaw it is and whether it reaches you.

### One field too many

API3 says that a property that isn't yours can't be seen or changed. Can someone sign up as an admin from the start? You try it on your own shop, on `localhost`: trying something like this on a system that isn't yours, without permission, is a crime.

<TryIt
  title="5. A sign-up that asks to be admin"
  cmd={`curl -s -w '\\n' --json '{"email":"jorge@example.com","password":"my bike is green and ten years old","role":"admin"}' localhost:3000/v2/accounts`}
  output={`{"id":7,"email":"jorge@example.com","role":"customer"}`}
>

Jorge is a `customer`. The new account's schema only knows `email` and `password`, Zod drops the rest, and the role comes from the table's `DEFAULT 'customer'`. If you get a `429`, you've already created 5 accounts this hour: wait, or restart the shop.

</TryIt>

### What a failed sign-in leaves in the log

<TryIt
  title="6. A wrong password"
  cmd={`curl -s -w '\\n' --json '{"email":"carmen@example.com","password":"not-mine"}' localhost:3000/v2/session`}
  output={`{"type":"https://shop.example/problems/invalid-credentials","title":"The email or the password is not correct","status":400,"detail":"Check both and try again"}`}
>

The `400` from lesson 2. Now look at the `npm start` terminal.

</TryIt>

In the shop's terminal there's one more line (we've replaced the computer's name with `laptop`):

```text
{"level":30,"time":1791953453862,"pid":181,"hostname":"laptop","requestId":"a58cb479-3942-4e2f-8a65-00245a46eb58","method":"POST","path":"/v2/session","status":400,"ms":26,"msg":"request"}
```

No trace of `not-mine`: good. But no trace of the email or the IP either.

<SelfCheck question="Challenge: with lines like that one, could you tell someone trying a thousand passwords on Carmen’s account apart from a thousand customers who each get it wrong once? What would you add to the line, and what never?">

No: both leave a thousand identical lines. Lesson 8's limiter counts by email and by IP, but in memory, without a trace.

An event line, with level `warn`, would add `failed sign-in`, the email tried and the IP. That way, “ten failures on one account” or “thirty from one IP” can become alerts. Never the password: a wrong one is often the right one with one letter changed, or the one from another site.

</SelfCheck>

## You have already seen it

- **The “new sign-in to your account” email** from Google or your bank: a security event recorded, and an alert to the only person who knows whether it was them.
- **npm's warnings when installing.** `npm install` runs `npm audit` on its own and shows the summary.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the Top 10 as a certificate.** “We comply with the OWASP Top 10” means nothing you can check; explaining how you cover each risk does.
- **Trusting a scanner.** It finds missing headers and packages with flaws, but it doesn't know that order 26 is Marta's.
- **Running `npm audit fix --force` blindly.** It can swap a package for an old or incompatible version.
- **Ignoring `npm audit`** because “there are always warnings.”
- **Logging the body of every request “to debug.”** The passwords go with it.
- **Logs without alerts:** the attack is written down, and nobody reads it.

## Summary

- OWASP is an open community with a nonprofit foundation behind it; its Top 10 gathers the most serious risks for web applications, and it isn't a certificate.
- The Top 10:2025 goes from broken access control (A01) to mishandling errors (A10, new); you've already closed almost all of them.
- APIs have their own Top 10 (2023), headed by BOLA.
- `secureHeaders()` adds the security headers, and `npm audit` tells you which packages have known flaws; before fixing, look at the severity and whether they reach production.
- A security log records what happened, to which account and from where; never passwords, sessions or tokens. And someone has to look at it.

- [OWASP API Security Top 10, 2023](https://api-security.owasp.org/editions/2023/en/0x11-t10/) (OWASP): the ten risks for APIs.
- [Logging Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Logging_Cheat_Sheet.html) (OWASP): which events to record and which data never.
- [HTTP Headers Cheat Sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/HTTP_Headers_Cheat_Sheet.html) (OWASP): each security header and its recommended value.
- [npm audit](https://docs.npmjs.com/cli/v11/commands/npm-audit/) (npm): all the command's options.
- [OWASP ASVS](https://owasp.org/www-project-application-security-verification-standard) (OWASP): the guide to verify an application point by point.
