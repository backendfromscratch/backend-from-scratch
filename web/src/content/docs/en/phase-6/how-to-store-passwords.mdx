## The problem

Imagine that, a year from now, someone walks off with a copy of the shop's database: a forgotten backup in a public place, a stolen laptop, or a <Term id="sql-injection">SQL injection</Term> like the one in [Phase 5](/en/phase-5/node-postgres/#pasting-the-value-into-the-text-sql-injection).

Marta is in the table, with her email address and her password. And Marta, like almost everyone, uses the same password for her email. With that row, whoever has it gets into her email, and from there asks for “I forgot my password” on all her other accounts.

The shop needs to check Marta's password every time she signs in. **How does it store it so it can check it, without anyone being able to read it, not even you?**

## The analogy

<Analogy>

Think of a smoothie. A banana, two strawberries and milk make a smoothie of a particular color and taste, and the same ingredients always make it the same. But from the smoothie you can't get back to the fruit.

The shop doesn't keep your fruit: it keeps your smoothie. When you come back, it blends what you bring and compares the color and the taste. If they match, it was your fruit.

So that nobody can prepare the smoothies of the most common fruits in advance, it adds to each one a pinch of a different spice, written on your glass. And it uses a blender that takes a while and fills the whole counter: for you it's a short wait; for whoever tries millions of fruits, an endless one.

<div slot="limits">

- Two smoothies made from almost the same fruit look alike. Two almost identical passwords give hashes that look nothing alike.
- The spice isn't secret: it's written on the glass. What it achieves is that every glass is different.
- Whoever steals the table doesn't separate the fruit: they try fruits until a smoothie matches. That's what the slow blender is for.

</div>

</Analogy>

## How it really works

### Storing it as it is: one leak shows them all

If the table stores Marta's password in plain text, **the first leak shows them all**. And not only in the shop: since many people reuse passwords, whoever has the list tries it on other services with the same email. It's called *credential stuffing*, and programs do it, by the million. Nobody on the inside should read them either: not you, and not your <Term id="log">logs</Term>.

### Encrypting it: whoever has the key has them all

<Term id="encryption">Encrypting</Term>, as in [Phase 0's TLS lesson](/en/phase-0/tls-and-https/#one-key-symmetric-encryption), looks like the solution, but encryption can be undone: that's its whole point. **Whoever has the key has every password at once.**

And the key has to be on the server, which uses it: whoever walks off with a copy of the server usually takes the `.env` with it.

But the shop never needs to read Marta's password: only to check that today's is the usual one. For that, something that can't be undone is enough.

### A hash: checking it without being able to read it

That's what a <Term id="hash">hash</Term> is: the fingerprint of some data, which can't be undone, as you saw in [“Git internals”](/en/phase-1/git-internals/#the-hash-the-fingerprint-of-the-data). The shop stores the hash of the password, and to check it, it computes the hash of the one that arrives and compares.

But not just any hash will do. Look at the one for `123456`, the most used password in the world, with SHA-256, the most common hash formula:

<TryIt
  title="The SHA-256 of 123456"
  cmd={`printf '%s' '123456' | shasum -a 256`}
  output={`8d969eef6ecad3c29a3a629280e686cf0c3f5d5a86aff3ca12020c923adc6c92  -`}
>

`printf '%s'` writes the text without a line break at the end, which would change the hash, and `shasum -a 256` computes its SHA-256. The `-` at the end means the text came through the <Term id="pipe">pipe</Term>, not from a file. On Linux and WSL, `sha256sum` does the same.

Search for that hash on the internet: you'll see it's `123456`.

</TryIt>

### SHA-256 is too fast

That hash has two problems.

**The first: the same password always gives the same hash.** If Carmen and Pablo use `123456`, their rows have the same text, and guessing one guesses both. Worse: there are **precomputed tables** with the hashes of billions of passwords, ready to be searched (*rainbow tables* are a compact way of storing them).

**The second: SHA-256 is fast.** It's made to summarize huge files in an instant, and here fast helps whoever attacks. Whoever steals the table doesn't undo any hash: they try passwords, one after another, and compare. A gaming graphics card computes billions of SHA-256 hashes per second.

In 2012, the hashes of about 6.5 million LinkedIn passwords leaked, computed [without a salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_LinkedIn_hack): with precomputed tables, many were guessed quickly.

Trying passwords against accounts or hashes that aren't yours, without permission, is a crime. In this lesson, everything is done against your shop, on `localhost`.

### The salt: the same password, two different hashes

The **<Term id="salt">salt</Term>** fixes the first problem. It's 16 random bytes, different for each password, mixed with it before computing the hash, and stored next to it.

- **Carmen and Pablo, with the same password, have different hashes,** because their salts are different.
- **Precomputed tables stop being useful:** you'd need one for each possible salt.

The salt isn't secret. And it doesn't fix the second problem: whoever attacks Marta tries with Marta's salt, just as fast.

### Slow on purpose: argon2id, bcrypt and scrypt

That's what **<Term id="password-hashing">password hashing</Term>** algorithms are for: slow on purpose, with adjustable slowness. Whoever signs in waits a few tens of milliseconds and doesn't notice. Whoever tries a billion passwords waits a billion times longer.

| Algorithm | Since | What makes it expensive | OWASP's minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| **argon2id** | 2015 | Time and memory | 19 MiB of memory, 2 passes, parallelism 1 |
| scrypt | 2009 | Time and memory | N = 2¹⁷, r = 8, p = 1 |
| bcrypt | 1999 | Time | Cost 10; it only reads the first 72 bytes |
| PBKDF2 | 2000 | Time (repetitions) | 600,000 iterations with SHA-256; only if a standard requires it |

**argon2id is the first choice of <Term id="owasp">OWASP</Term>,** the web security community of lesson 9. argon2, its family, won a public password algorithm competition in 2015. PBKDF2, a hash repeated thousands of times, is the one you used in the [event loop lesson](/en/phase-3/node-event-loop/#calculating-does-block) of Phase 3.

Memory is the key. A graphics card has thousands of small cores, but little memory for each one. If every attempt needs 19 MiB, the memory runs out long before the cores do.

### argon2id in Node, without installing anything

Node has included argon2 since version 24.7, in `node:crypto`. The shop's parameters, in `src/passwords.ts`:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/24-passwords/src/passwords.ts" region="parameters" />

- **`memory: 19_456`**: the memory, in blocks of 1 KiB: 19 MiB. The `_` only separates the digits.
- **`passes: 2`**: how many passes it makes over that memory.
- **`parallelism: 1`**: how many parts it splits the work into, which could be computed at the same time.
- **`tagLength: 32`**: the hash comes out at 32 bytes.
- **`promisify(argon2)`**: `argon2` reports back when it's done by calling a <Term id="function">function</Term> you pass it; `promisify` turns it into one that returns a <Term id="promise">promise</Term>, so you can use `await`. It computes in a helper thread, and the server keeps serving everyone else.

**`crypto.argon2` arrived as experimental,** and the [Node 26 documentation](https://nodejs.org/api/crypto.html#cryptoargon2algorithm-parameters-callback) no longer marks it that way. The project uses Node 26. With an older Node, the [`@node-rs/argon2`](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@node-rs/argon2) package does the same, with argon2 already compiled for each system.

### The PHC format: everything needed in one text

`hashPassword` computes the hash and stores everything in a single text:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/24-passwords/src/passwords.ts" region="hash" />

- **`randomBytes(SALT_BYTES)`**: the salt, 16 random bytes, new in every call.
- **`normalize('NFKC')`**: `é` can be written as a single letter or as an `e` plus an accent. Normalized, the same password always gives the same bytes.
- **The `return`** joins everything with `$`, in the PHC format, the one almost every argon2 library uses.

Marta's, which you'll create in “Try it,” piece by piece:

```
$argon2id$v=19$m=19456,t=2,p=1$p0YG0BI1Ezmt3sR0j6EO9g$xmgmXcaLwoY/GWPRpQVbNaC/bc4XeVdq5g1aCnRYMtc
```

| Piece | What it is |
|---|---|
| `argon2id` | The algorithm |
| `v=19` | The argon2 version, 1.3: it's written `0x13` in hexadecimal, which is 19 |
| `m=19456,t=2,p=1` | Memory, passes and threads |
| `p0YG0BI1Ezmt3sR0j6EO9g` | The salt: 16 bytes in base64, without the `=` padding |
| `xmgmXcaLwoY/…nRYMtc` | The hash: 32 bytes in base64 |

With the parameters inside, tomorrow you can raise them for new passwords, and the old ones are checked with their own. An old one can only be updated when its owner signs in: it's the only moment the shop has the password.

### Checking: the same computation and a constant-time comparison

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/24-passwords/src/passwords.ts" region="verify" />

- **`stored.split('$')`** separates the pieces, and the computation is the one in `hashPassword`, with the stored salt and parameters.
- **`timingSafeEqual`** compares taking the same time whether it matches or not, like the webhooks' signature in [Phase 4](/en/phase-4/webhooks-idempotency/#checking-the-signature-the-body-as-it-is-in-constant-time). A normal comparison stops at the first different byte, and by measuring how long it takes, someone can work out the result bit by bit.

### The rules that help and the ones that don't

The usual rules, “one capital letter, one number and one symbol, and change it every three months,” push people to `Password1!`, then to `Password2!`, and to a sticky note. NIST, the United States standards institute, withdrew them in its [SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html) guide, from 2025:

| Helps | Doesn't help |
|---|---|
| A minimum of 15 characters if the password is used on its own, without a second factor: a sentence will do | Requiring capital letters, numbers or symbols (NIST forbids it) |
| Accepting at least 64, with spaces, accents and emojis | A short maximum, like 16 |
| Rejecting common, leaked or predictable passwords, and saying why | Changing it every so often: only if there are signs it has leaked |
| Allowing pasting and password managers | Hints and security questions (“your first pet?”) |

In the shop:

<ProjectCode file="shop-api/24-passwords/src/passwords.ts" region="rules" />

- **`MIN_LENGTH` and `MAX_LENGTH`**: from 15 to 128 characters.
- **`COMMON`** reads `src/common-passwords.txt`: the ones with 15 characters or more that are a pattern (keyboard rows, digits in a row) among the 100,000 most used, a list from the UK's National Cyber Security Centre. With `toLowerCase()`, `QWERTY…` and `qwerty…` count the same.
- **`[...password.normalize('NFKC')].length`** counts real characters: for `length`, an emoji takes up two.

A real shop would check many more, for example with [Pwned Passwords](https://haveibeenpwned.com/Passwords), a service that tells you whether a password has appeared in a leak without you sending it the password.

## You have already seen it

- **“I forgot my password.”** A well-made site sends you a link to set a new one, never your password: it doesn't have it. If one ever emails it to you, it stores it in plain text or encrypted.
- **The leaked password warning** from your browser or your password manager: it checks yours against known leaks.

## Common mistakes

- **Encrypting passwords,** or converting them to base64, which isn't even encryption: both can be undone.
- **Using a fast hash,** MD5, SHA-1 or SHA-256, even with a salt.
- **One fixed salt for everyone, or the email as the salt.** The salt is random and different in every hash.
- **Comparing with `===`.** Use `timingSafeEqual`.
- **Using `argon2Sync` in a route:** it blocks the server while it computes.
- **Writing the request body to the log,** or returning the hash in the API.

## Summary

- A password is never stored: not in plain text, because one leak shows them all, and not encrypted, because whoever has the key has them all.
- A hash is stored, which can't be undone. To check the password, it's computed again and compared in constant time.
- A fast hash like SHA-256 lets attackers try billions of passwords per second. The salt, random for each one, makes precomputed tables useless.
- argon2id is slow on purpose and needs memory. The stored text (PHC) carries the algorithm, the parameters, the salt and the hash.
- NIST: 15 characters at least and a list of common passwords; no required symbols and no expiry.

>

<QuizOption text="They can still sign in: each stored text carries its parameters, and checking uses those." correct>

`m=19456` is inside the text. New hashes use 46 MiB, and each old one can be recomputed when its owner signs in.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="Nobody can sign in until they change their password, because the old hashes no longer match.">

They wouldn't match if `verifyPassword` used the new parameters, but it uses the ones stored with each hash.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="All the stored hashes have to be recomputed with a script, the same night as the change.">

It can't be done: computing the new hash needs the password, and the shop doesn't have it. It only has it for a moment, when someone signs in.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>

<QuizQuestion
  question="You click “I forgot my password” on a site, and you get an email with your usual password. What does that tell you?"
  review="Encrypting it: whoever has the key has them all"
>

<QuizOption text="Nothing bad: it must have got it from its hash just to send it to you.">

You can't get a password from a hash. If it sends it to you, it stores something that can be read.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="That it stores it in plain text or encrypted: it can be read." correct>

A site that stores a hash can't send it to you: it sends you a link to set a new one. Change that password everywhere you use it.

</QuizOption>

<QuizOption text="That it stores it properly, because the email travels encrypted with TLS.">

TLS protects the journey, not what the server stores. And the email stays in your inbox, in plain text.

</QuizOption>

</QuizQuestion>
